Fractopia's activities are now real games, and no two rounds are the same

Every Fractions activity, from Crumb Meadow to Sugar Summit, is now a small animated game instead of a question with a row of buttons. You serve pizzas to a customer, send a frog hopping along lily pads, pop balloons, hitch fraction cars to a train, or dial a recipe into a fraction oven.

Fresh numbers every time. The questions used to come from a fixed list, so a child could meet the same one twice. Now every question is made on the spot, so each play has new fractions, new totals and new shapes.

Dressed for the place. Each continent has its own colours, scenery and friendly mascot, and each country picks its own treat and palette, so neighbouring activities look different.

A fix, too. On the welcome screen of an activity, the "back" link was white on a white button and could not be read. It is now black.

The same treatment reaches three more worlds

Fractopia was the test run, and it worked, so the games now travel. Numeria (numbers), Chronia (time) and Mintopia (money), 273 activities from Kindergarten to Grade 6, have been rebuilt on a shared set of nine game styles, and the other worlds can follow without starting from scratch.

Every world feels like its own place. In Numeria you pop numbers like balloons and hop a frog along rivers that are number lines. In Chronia you ring bells, turn the hands of a clock tower and load a time train. In Mintopia you drop coins into a till, type prices on a cash register and hunt for bargains. Each country within a world picks its own colours, mascot and answer style, so two neighbouring activities never look the same.

More ways to play. Instead of tapping a button under a question, children now type on a keypad, sort cards into bins, put coins on a tray, set a clock, order cards on a train or catch every leap year as it floats by.

Always new questions. Counting patterns, clock times, prices, change and discounts are made fresh each time, in English and in French, with the right number words, thousands separators, decimal commas and money formats for each language.

Blockopolis joins in

The fourth world is Blockopolis, the city of place value, with 93 activities from Kindergarten to Grade 6. Children build numbers from ones, tens and hundreds blocks, fill ten frames, trade ten for one, move a digit up or down a place, line up decimals, and round to the nearest ten or to a number of significant figures. It took the same nine game styles and a new set of question makers, with no new game engine, which is the point of building it once.

Primoria, the crystal caves of number theory

The fifth world to get the treatment is Primoria, where 72 activities from Kindergarten to Grade 6 now run on the same nine game styles. In the lower caves children pair up pebbles and sort numbers into "pairs up" and "one left over", then learn to call them even and odd. Further down they hop a frog along the multiples of 6, catch every factor of 36 as it floats by, type the missing partner in 24 = 4 × ?, and sort cards into factor, multiple or neither.

Primes, powers and roots. The deeper caves ask children to catch the primes among a dozen drifting numbers, decide by the last digits whether a number divides by 2, 5 or 10, and later by the tests for 3, 4, 6 and 9. They type squares and cubes, squeeze a square root between two whole numbers, and find a highest common factor or lowest common multiple by reading prime factors, the way two buses leaving together meet again.

Always new, in two languages. Every number is rolled on the spot, so a child who catches the factors of 36 today may be catching those of 48 tomorrow. The words, the days of the week and the way numbers are written follow the child's language, in English and in French.

Operon Isles, where plus, minus, times and share meet

The sixth world is the Operon Isles, the islands of the four operations, and it is the biggest so far: 166 activities from Kindergarten to Grade 6, all running on the same nine game styles. The youngest children act out a story on Plus Pier, count what comes and what goes, pick the right sign, and pair numbers that make ten. By Minus Marina they are splitting numbers into tens and ones, jumping along a number line, and spotting which facts belong in the same family.

From carrying to long division. Further out, children add and subtract in columns and decide whether they need to trade, hunt for the one answer with a slip, and judge which result is even possible by estimating first. They hop along times tables, catch every number in the 7 table, and check a division with a multiplication. In the Long Lagoon they split a product into place-value parts, divide by chunks, decide whether a remainder should be rounded up, left out or shared, and line up decimal points.

Order, brackets and powers. The last islands ask what to work out first in 8 + 6 × 3, whether Mia is right about (4 + 6) × 3, where brackets change an answer, and how a product like (x + 2)(x + 3) looks as an area. Every sum is made fresh each time, so a child who solves 7 × 18 today meets a different one tomorrow, with the numbers written the English or French way.

Proportia, where markets and recipes scale

Proportia is the world of decimals, percent and ratio, and its 67 activities from Grade 3 to Grade 6 now play like the others. Children compare 3.45 with 3.5 and find the winner of a decimal duel, hop along a number line that zooms from tenths to hundredths, and match one half to 0.5 to 50%. Further on they work out 15% of 80 by building it from 10% and 5%, find a sale price, and share a prize in the ratio 3 to 1.

Where it gets interesting. In Grade 6 the questions turn to why a price that goes up 10% and then down 10% does not come back to where it started, how to undo a discount, and how two buses or two mixes compare. Every price, recipe and score is made fresh, and French uses the right spacing, so 25 % and 3,40 $ look the way a French-speaking child expects.

Chancery, a fairground of chance

The 43 probability activities now run as a fairground. Young children decide whether things are sure, maybe or never, pull counters from a bag and say how likely a colour is. Later they list every outcome of a die or two coins, judge whether a spinner is fair, write chances as fractions, count the 36 cells of a two-dice grid, and spot a rigged die from its tally.

Zoomia, a racing world for quick thinking

Zoomia holds the 111 mental-fluency activities, from doubles and halves in Kindergarten to bridging through ten, adding nine by adding ten and taking one back, times tables, estimating, and checking an answer by working it backwards. By Grade 6 children recall squares, double their way up the chessboard grains, and make Fermi guesses.

Patternia, music, machines and balance

Patternia has 103 activities about patterns and algebra. Children continue clap-and-stomp patterns, find the missing number that balances an equation, run numbers through function machines, work out the rule of a sequence, and in Grade 6 collect like terms, expand brackets and spot the slip in a worked line.

Metrovia, a harbour workshop of measures

The 136 measurement activities cover comparing and ordering sizes, reading a ruler, a kitchen scale, a jug and a thermometer, converting units, perimeter, area, volume, circles, and speed, distance and time. A frog marks the reading on each scale, so the child reads the instrument instead of a number in a sentence.

Geomeria, shapes and angles

Geomeria's 158 activities start with naming shapes and solids, counting sides and corners, then move on to properties of polygons, lines of symmetry, angles of every kind, parallel lines, triangles, circles, nets of a cube and the family tree of quadrilaterals. Children also work out missing angles on a line, around a point and inside a triangle, and name the reason for each step.

Wayfinder Reach, maps, compasses and robot routes

The 83 position and movement activities include robot routes on a small grid, compass directions and turns, grid references, coordinates in all four quadrants, translating, reflecting and rotating a point, and scale on a map. Even the youngest ones, who learn on, under and next to, now see a fresh picture each time.

Chartlands, surveys that collect, chart and compare

Chartlands has 98 data activities. Children sort and count, read pictograms and bar charts whose symbols stand for more than one, read tally marks, line plots and line graphs, find the mean, median, mode and range, read a pie chart, and in Grade 6 describe a scatter graph and tell a correlation from a cause. They also learn to spot a misleading graph or an unfair sample.

Puzzleton, a detective city

The last world, Puzzleton, holds 107 problem-solving activities. Younger children act out stories and say how they know; older ones choose the operation, draw a bar model, list every outfit, judge whether an answer is sensible, find a counterexample, and test whether a claim is proven or only lucky. Every story takes its numbers and its objects from a fresh draw, so a child who solves a coin story today meets a marble story tomorrow.

Every world, now. With these nine, all sixteen worlds of the Explorer Atlas are games with fresh questions in English and French. The rooms still have the same shapes and the same level of difficulty by grade; only the questions are never the same twice.

Countries you can recognise, grouped by skill

On the map, each grade of a world is a continent and each activity is a country. Until now the countries had invented names, and the same name could turn up in two different grades, which made them hard to tell apart. Now every country is named after its activity, such as "Comparing Decimals", and no two countries in a world share a name. If two activities happen to share a title, the later grade's one carries its grade, as in "Decimal Duel · G4".

The syllabus, readable from the page. A continent page now groups its countries under the skill they practise, in the order the syllabus teaches them, with the skill title as a heading. A parent can read what Grade 4 covers in a world straight from the page, and a child still taps a country to play.

A printable mental-math sheet for every level

The daily mental-math sheet used to be one sheet for everyone. Now there is one for each level, from kindergarten to grade 6, with seven levels in all. Kindergarten gets additions and subtractions within 20. Grade 1 goes up to 100, grade 2 to 1000 with multiplication added. Grades 3 and 4 keep the original four-operation sheet, and a fourth level adds more multiplication. The last two levels bring in negative numbers, and in the sixth, decimals with one or two digits after the comma, such as 0.1, 0.01 or −3.75.

Negatives and decimals, carefully. A negative number is written in brackets, as in (−7) + 12 or (−3) × (−4), so a child can see where the sign belongs. Decimal answers are exact, and every division comes out even, so a child never meets a quotient that goes on forever.

One sheet per level, each day. Each level gets its own sheet for the day, and it stays the same all day. Press New sheet for another.

Pick a grade, print a clean page. The level buttons have given way to a single grade drop-down, so the page is less crowded. The sheet is titled with just its grade, for example "Worksheet (grade 6)", and when printed it no longer carries the date, title and web address that browsers add at the top and bottom of the page.

## Every activity is now a country, and every world has a map

Open a world and you no longer meet a plain list: you meet an atlas. Every activity is now a fictive country with a name of its own, built from the world's theme and the land, or grade, it belongs to. "Tally Republic" is a Numeria country in the Kindergarten land, and "Crumb Empire" is a Fractopia country in Grade 4. Names are never repeated anywhere in the atlas, so a child can say "I finished Crumb Empire" and everyone knows exactly where. The real activity name is still there, on the country's welcome screen, so a parent can see what is being practised.

**A map at the top of every world.** Above the list of lands sits a small map with seven islands, one per land, and one pin for each activity. A little boat floats at the land the child is working on, so the next step is easy to find. Each world draws its map differently, with its own layout, colours and scenery, so the maps feel as different as the worlds.

**Explore it with a finger or a mouse.** Children can drag the map to move around, press + and − to zoom, or tap a land to zoom straight to it. A caption under the map names the country or land under the pointer, with the stars earned there. Tapping a pin opens that activity. Keyboard users can reach every land and pin, and the controls are labelled in English and French.
