Real counting vs reciting numbers: what to look for before school

Many parents are proud, rightly, when a three-year-old recites the numbers up to twenty in a clear, confident voice. It is a real achievement. But reciting numbers and counting are two different skills, and teachers of kindergarten will tell you that the gap between them is one of the most important things to notice before school starts. This article explains what real counting involves, how to see whether your child has it, and what you can do at home, with nothing more than a bowl of buttons or a row of apples, to help it grow.

Written for children aged 3 to 6.

Two skills that look the same from across the room

When a toddler says "one, two, three, four, five, six, seven", they are doing something like singing a song. The words come in a fixed order, and the order has to be learned by heart. Researchers call this the number word sequence. It is useful, and it is the raw material for counting, but on its own it tells us nothing about quantity. A child can recite to thirty and still have no idea that "seven" means this many.

Counting a set of objects is a coordination of several things at once:

  • saying the number words in the correct order,
  • pointing to or touching each object exactly once, one word for each object (this is called one-to-one correspondence),
  • keeping track of which objects have already been counted and which have not,
  • and, crucially, understanding that the last word spoken tells you how many there are in all.

That last idea is known as the cardinal principle, and it is the moment counting becomes mathematics. Children usually reach it somewhere between three and a half and five years old, but the range is wide and a child who gets there later is not "behind" in any meaningful way. What matters is that they get there with understanding, not by rote.

How to see what your child really knows

You do not need a worksheet. A few relaxed moments are enough. Try these, one at a time and never as a test:

The "how many?" check. Put five raisins on a plate and ask your child to count them. Watch carefully. Do they touch each raisin once? Do they skip one, or count one twice? When they finish, ask, "So how many raisins are there?" A child who has grasped the cardinal principle answers "five" straight away. A child who is still reciting will often start counting again from one, because to them the question does not match what they just did.

The "give me" check. Place a pile of ten blocks in front of your child and say, "Can you give me three blocks?" This is harder than counting, because the child has to stop at the right moment. A child who grabs a handful and hands it over is guessing. A child who counts "one, two, three" while moving blocks and then stops has understood that the number word names a quantity.

The rearranged-row check. Line up six counters in a neat row, count them together, and then spread the same counters in a wide circle. Ask, "Are there still six, or are there more now?" Young children are often convinced the long row has more. Knowing that the number stays the same however the objects are arranged is a milestone sometimes called conservation of number. Do not worry if your child is not sure yet; it develops with experience.

The "start from here" check. Count five objects, then add one more and ask how many there are now. A child who counts everything again from one is still building. A child who says "six" without recounting, or who counts on from five, is becoming flexible, and that flexibility leads straight to addition later on.

Common stumbling blocks, and what they mean

Counting faster than pointing. A child races through the number words while their finger lags behind, so the words and the objects do not match. Slow the game down, and let them move each object across the table as it is counted, so counted and not-yet-counted objects are in two separate piles.

Skipping or repeating a number word. "One, two, three, five, six." This is very common and usually reflects the number sequence not being fully learned yet. Counting rhymes, songs and stairs help here.

Getting stuck at the teens. In English the numbers between thirteen and nineteen do not follow a simple pattern, and many children find them hard. In French the irregular stretches are eleven to sixteen (onze to seize) and then the seventies, eighties and nineties (soixante-dix, quatre-vingts, quatre-vingt-dix). This is a language issue, not a mathematical weakness, and it improves with repetition rather than pressure.

Counting correctly but not answering "how many". This is the child who needs more experience with the cardinal principle. Make a habit of finishing every counting game with the same question: "So how many are there?"

Building real counting at home

The best practice is the kind that happens while you are doing something else.

Count things that matter to your child. Stairs on the way up, grapes on a plate, toy cars lined up at the door, steps to the mailbox. A real reason to know how many ("one grape for you, one for me") beats any drill.

Let them touch. For children under five, counting by pointing from across the room is nearly impossible. Letting them move each object keeps their fingers, eyes and voice in step. A muffin tin with twelve cups is an excellent counting tool: one object per cup makes one-to-one correspondence obvious.

Count out loud together, then let go. Start by counting with your child, in a steady rhythm. As they gain confidence, count silently and let them take over, stepping back in only when they lose the thread.

Vary the objects and the layout. Counting a row is the easiest case. Try a pile, a circle, objects scattered across a table. Each layout asks the child to organise their counting, which is where much of the learning happens.

Ask "how many?" and also "how do you know?" When your child answers, ask them to show you. Explaining builds understanding and helps you see what they are really doing.

Make mistakes together. Count wrongly on purpose and let your child catch you: "One, two, three, five…" Spotting an error is a sign of real knowledge, and children love correcting a grown-up.

Small numbers first, and no rush

It is far better for a child to count confidently and meaningfully up to ten than to recite to a hundred. Stay with small numbers until the cardinal principle is secure, then extend gradually. Counting to twenty, then thirty, is mostly a matter of learning the number words and the patterns they follow; the underlying idea does not change.

If your child is four or five and still seems to be reciting rather than counting, there is no reason for alarm. Keep the games playful, short and frequent, a few minutes at a time. Most children arrive at the idea suddenly, as though a light has been switched on, and then enjoy counting everything in sight.

A short checklist for before school

By the time children start kindergarten, teachers are glad to see that a child can:

  • count a small set of objects (up to about five to ten) by touching each once,
  • say how many there are when they finish,
  • give you a requested number of objects, up to about five,
  • recite the number words in order to about ten, and often beyond,
  • and show curiosity about numbers in the world around them.

None of these needs special equipment. They need time, attention and a willingness to count the ordinary things of the day together. That is where real counting begins.
