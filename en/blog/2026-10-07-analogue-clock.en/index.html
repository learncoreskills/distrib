Telling the time on an analogue clock, step by step

In a world of phones and digital displays, it is fair to ask whether children still need to read an analogue clock. Many schools still teach it, and for good reason. An analogue clock shows time as a quantity: you can see how much of the hour has gone and how much is left. Reading one draws on counting by fives, on fractions (half past, quarter to), and on the idea that two hands move at different speeds. It is also simply a skill a child is likely to meet in classrooms, stations and kitchens for years. This article describes a sensible order for teaching it, common stumbling blocks, and games for practice at home.

Written for children aged 6 to 9.

Before the clock: time as experience

Telling time begins long before a child can read a clock face. Children first need language and experience of time: before and after, morning and evening, yesterday and tomorrow, "a long time" and "a short time". Daily routines (breakfast, school, bath, bedtime) give time a shape. Talking through the day, "after lunch we'll go to the park", builds the foundation.

Next comes the idea that time can be measured. A sand timer, a kitchen timer or counting seconds aloud gives young children a feel for how long a minute is. "Can you hop for one minute? Can you tidy your toys in five?" These tasks are fun, and they show that a minute and an hour are different amounts.

Step 1: the hour hand and "o'clock"

Start with the short hand only. Show a clock where the minute hand points straight up at 12 and the hour hand points to a number: that is "o'clock". Children first learn to read and set these. The vocabulary is simple: "It's three o'clock."

It helps to use a real, large clock with movable hands. A cheap teaching clock, or a paper plate with a split-pin and two card hands, works well. Let your child turn the hands, and notice that when the long hand goes all the way round, the short hand moves on by only one number.

Step 2: half past

The next step is half past: the minute hand points straight down at 6, and the hour hand sits between two numbers. This is the first time the hour hand does not point exactly at a number, which confuses some children. Helpful guidance: "At half past three, the short hand is halfway between 3 and 4. It has gone past 3 but not yet reached 4." Linking this to fractions (a half hour) is natural: "the minute hand has gone half way round the clock".

Step 3: quarter past and quarter to

The minute hand pointing at 3 shows a quarter of the way round: "quarter past". Pointing at 9 shows three quarters of the way round, which is a quarter before the next hour: "quarter to". A pizza cut into quarters makes a good model. Children can see that a clock is simply a circle cut into fractions.

Step 4: counting minutes in fives

Now comes the biggest step: the minute hand and the numbers. Each number on the clock face stands for five minutes, so 1 is five minutes past, 2 is ten past, 3 is fifteen past (quarter past), and so on. Children need to be able to count in fives to read the minutes, so check that skip counting by five is secure. Count round the clock together: "5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30...".

Once your child can count around the clock in fives, they can read times like "ten past four" or "twenty-five to nine". Explore both sides of the hour: past when the minute hand is on the right side of the clock (up to 30 minutes), and to when it is on the left side. Many children find "to" harder, because they have to think about how many minutes remain until the next hour. Practise with phrases such as "five to six means five minutes before six".

Step 5: minutes between the fives

The last step is reading single minutes using the small tick marks. A child who is confident with fives can count on from the nearest five: "the minute hand is just past the 4, which is 20 minutes, then 3 more: 23 minutes past four". This is a natural time to link to bridging and mental addition.

Digital and analogue together

Children also need to link analogue and digital forms. At the same moment, an analogue clock shows hands pointing to 4 and 12, and a digital clock shows 4:00. Reading both together, and noticing "the part after the colon is the minutes", helps cement the connection. For this reason, a good practice is to have both in the house and ask your child to match them.

Be aware of the 24-hour clock too. In many countries, and in French-speaking Canada, 24-hour time is common in everyday life (as in "il est 15 h 30"). An analogue clock with 1 to 12 does not show it directly, but a child can learn that 15 h is 3 in the afternoon by subtracting 12.

Common stumbling blocks

Mixing up the hands. Remind your child: the short hand is the hour (it is the short, important one that moves slowly) and the long hand is the minutes. Colour-coding them can help.

Reading the hour hand wrongly at half past. Remind them that the hour hand has to be past the number but not yet at the next one.

Confusing 5 and 25, or 6 as "six minutes". The number on the clock face is not the number of minutes. This is the part where the "count in fives" rhythm matters most.

Quarter to being treated as "quarter past". Use pizza slices or the clock's right and left sides.

Games for home

Clock at the door. Put a clock where your child can see it, and make time a topic: "It's quarter to eight; we leave at eight. How many minutes do we have?"

Set the time. Call out a time and have your child set the hands on a practice clock. Then swap: you set, they read.

Time detective. Say "I'm a time. The short hand is halfway between 4 and 5 and the long hand is at 6. What time am I?" Detective games develop reasoning.

Daily schedule. Make a poster of the day with analogue clocks beside key events: breakfast, school, bath.

Time stories. Ask: "If it is 3 o'clock now, what time will it be in one hour? In two?" Elapsed time follows naturally once reading the clock is secure.

Patience and practice

Telling time is a skill that develops across several grades, and most children need many short, regular encounters rather than a single lesson. If your child struggles, step back one stage and consolidate it. Counting by fives, the idea of half and quarter, and everyday talk about time all support the learning.

Before long, you will find your child glancing at the kitchen clock and announcing "It's ten to six", and you will have given them a skill that combines counting, fractions and a feel for how the day unfolds.
