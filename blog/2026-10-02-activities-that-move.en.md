# Activities that move: apples you can count, frogs that hop

Until today, a counting question in the app was a row of emoji characters glued together, like
"🍎🍎🍎🍎🍎🍎🍎 + 🍎🍎🍎", and a child had to squint at it like text. Now those questions are
drawn as little scenes instead. Apples pop onto a plate one by one, dots appear in a ten-frame
and then vanish, and a frog hops along a number line. When the answer is right, the screen
bursts into confetti.

**Why drawing matters, not just decoration**

Young children don't count text, they count things. A long string of identical emoji is hard to
count even for an adult: there is nothing to anchor the eye, and it's easy to lose your place
halfway. So the new scenes are laid out the way a teacher would lay out counters on a table:

```
  Before:   🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓🍓

  Now:      ┌─────────────────────┐
            │ 🍓  🍓  🍓  🍓  🍓  │   rows of five, so "ten and three"
            │ 🍓  🍓  🍓  🍓  🍓  │   can be seen, not just counted
            │ 🍓  🍓  🍓          │
            └─────────────────────┘
```

Rows of five are a deliberate choice. They let a child see "five, ten, and three more" at a
glance, which is the first step from counting one by one towards understanding tens.

**Three scenes, each matched to a skill**

- **Counters on a plate**, for counting, for "how many altogether / how many left" with small
  amounts, and for "which group has more". Objects pop in one after another, so a child can
  follow along. In a comparison, the two groups sit side by side with a bright "VS" between
  them.
- **A ten-frame**, the two-row box of ten cells that classrooms everywhere use. It's used for
  *recognising* small amounts without counting them, a skill teachers call subitizing. The dots
  appear for a moment and then disappear, so the child has to *see* "four" rather than tap
  through "one, two, three, four". Once they answer, the dots come back so they can check
  themselves.
- **A number line with a frog**, for counting in twos, fives or tens. The known numbers are
  joined by little arcs that draw themselves one hop at a time. The frog waits on the last one,
  and a yellow "?" bubble marks where it will land next. On a right answer the frog jumps onto
  the bubble and the number appears. On a wrong answer the number is revealed too, so the
  question still teaches something.

**Feedback that feels like a game**

Every question now slides gently into place. A correct answer gets a short burst of confetti, and
the objects in the scene do a little bounce. A wrong answer gets a soft wiggle of the card. There
is no red flash and no buzzer, just a nudge, followed by the right answer.

Some children (and adults) find on-screen motion uncomfortable, and most phones and computers
have a "reduce motion" setting for this. When it's switched on, the app now respects it
completely. There's no confetti, wiggle or hopping, just the same scenes, drawn still. The one
thing that stays is the disappearing ten-frame, because that's part of the exercise, not
decoration.

**Tap the chicks: answering inside the picture**

Later the same day, we looked hard at one of the youngest activities, "Which has more?", and
weren't happy with it. It showed two rows of objects as two big buttons, so a four-year-old was
really picking a button, not comparing groups. It now works the way a teacher would do it on a
table. The balloons and chicks are tipped out together, all jumbled, into a bigger play area. The
child taps a chick (or a balloon) to say "these ones". Children who use a keyboard or a screen
reader get one big round button per kind of object.

The best part comes after the answer, right or wrong. Every object glides out of the jumble into
two neat lines, a balloon above each chick, like children pairing up in the playground:

```
  🎈 🎈 🎈 🎈 🎈 🎈
  🐤 🐤 🐤 🐤 🐤 🐤 🐤 🐤 🐤   <- these three have no partner, and they glow
```

The chicks without a partner light up. That's the idea of "more" made visible: not "the row
looks longer", but "some are left over when you pair them up", which is exactly how early-years
teachers introduce it. A wrong answer becomes a small lesson rather than a red cross.

**A frog that counts (and sometimes falls in)**

The last activity we reworked today was "Count in Order": does the child know the counting words
come in a fixed order? It used to show four near-identical buttons like "1 2 3 3 4 5" and
"1 2 4 5", which only an adult reader could tell apart. Before redesigning it we asked two
questions: what would a teacher want a child to practise here, and what can a three-year-old
actually use on a phone?

The answer is a pond. Each possible counting is a lane, and each lane has a frog on the bank and a
row of lily pads with one number per pad. For short counts the pads also carry dots, so the
number can be seen as well as read. The child taps a lane, and that lane's frog sets off:

```
  right lane:  🐸 → 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5          every pad lights up, the lane bounces
  wrong lane:  🐸 → 1 → 2 → 4 💦                the frog slips where 3 should have been
                         ( 3 )                  a ghost pad shows the missing number
```

When the counting is wrong, the frog stumbles at the exact spot where it goes wrong: a skipped
number, a number said twice, or two numbers in the wrong order. Then the frog in the correct lane
hops the right sequence, one lit pad at a time. The mistake isn't hidden behind a red cross. The
child sees it happen and then watches the right way.

**Built once, reused everywhere**

The bigger change is behind the scenes. Instead of drawing each activity by hand, the app now has
a small "activity engine": a shared set of building blocks (plates of counters, ten-frames, number
lines) that any activity can describe and the app knows how to draw and animate. Moving an
activity onto it is now a matter of saying *what* to show ("three apples plus two apples"),
not *how* to draw it. That's why three activities could switch over today. Many more can follow,
and new building blocks are already planned: base-ten blocks for place value, a ruler for
measuring, a clock face, fraction bars, and later, activities where children drag and tap
objects themselves instead of typing an answer.
