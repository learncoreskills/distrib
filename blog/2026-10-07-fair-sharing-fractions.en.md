# Fractions start with fair sharing

For many adults, fractions are where mathematics started to feel difficult. Rules such as "flip and multiply" or "find a common denominator" were memorised without much sense of what they meant. Research on how children learn suggests that this struggle often begins at the very start, when fractions are introduced as symbols to manipulate rather than as quantities to understand. The good news is that there is a natural, joyful starting point, and children already know it: **sharing fairly**. This article describes how to use sharing to give fractions meaning, and what to look for as understanding develops.

*Written for children aged 6 to 9.*

## Children already know fractions without knowing it

Ask a four-year-old to share a biscuit with a friend and watch carefully. They will usually break it in two and compare the pieces. If one is bigger, there will be objections. Already they have understood the central idea of a fraction: **equal parts of a whole**. They may not use the word "half", but they know what fairness means.

The first mathematical goal is to attach the language and ideas to that experience: a half is one of two *equal* parts; a quarter is one of four equal parts; a third is one of three equal parts. The word "equal" is not decoration. A piece that is cut into two unequal pieces does not make halves. Children often need to learn this explicitly, because a typical school picture of "half a circle" can lead them to think that any two pieces make halves.

## Equal sharing, step by step

**Share objects you can cut.** A sandwich, a pizza, a piece of paper, a strip of ribbon, a chocolate bar. Ask: "How can we share this so it is fair for two people?" Let your child try, and then check: do the two pieces fit exactly on top of one another? If not, why not?

**Move to more people.** "Now four people want some." Folding paper in half, and then in half again, shows that a half of a half is a quarter. Folding it into three is harder, which is itself an interesting discovery: not all fair sharing is equally easy.

**Share collections too.** Fractions apply to groups, not only single objects. "Eight grapes, shared fairly between four people: how many each?" This links fractions and division, and shows that a quarter of eight is two.

**Make the whole visible.** Ask: "Half of *what*?" Half of a small pizza is not the same amount as half of a large one. This surprises many children, and it points to something deep: a fraction is a relationship between a part and its whole.

## Words before symbols

It is tempting to write ½ on a page straight away. But it can wait. Let your child talk about "half", "a quarter", "one of three equal parts" and see the pieces first. When you do introduce the notation, link it to the meaning: the bottom number tells how many equal parts the whole is cut into; the top number tells how many of those parts we have. So ¾ means three of four equal parts.

Many children find it odd that the *bigger* bottom number gives the *smaller* piece. A quarter is smaller than a half, even though 4 is bigger than 2. This is an excellent topic for conversation, and a pizza or a strip of paper resolves it better than any rule: the more people share, the smaller each share becomes.

## Common misconceptions to watch for

*Unequal parts counted as halves.* Check for equal size, always.

*Treating the numerator and denominator as separate numbers.* A child may say ¼ is "bigger than ½" because 4 is bigger than 2. Return to the concrete: "If you share a pizza among four, or among two, who gets more?"

*Fractions as just numbers on a page.* If a child cannot say where ⅓ is or what it looks like, the symbol has no meaning yet. Move back to objects and drawings.

*Thinking that a fraction is always less than one.* Later, fractions like 3/2 will appear. Children who think of "one half, two halves, three halves" as counting halves find this much easier to accept.

## Fractions on a number line

Once children are comfortable with pieces of shapes, introduce the number line. Place 0 and 1 at the ends of a strip of paper and ask where ½ sits. Fold the strip in half to check. Then mark quarters. This shows a really important idea: fractions are *numbers*, with places on the line between the whole numbers, not just pieces of pies. That understanding makes comparing, adding and ordering fractions possible later.

## Practical activities at home

**Cooking and baking.** Measuring cups, with their ½ and ¼ markings, are fraction tools. "We need half a cup. Can we do that with the quarter-cup?" Two quarters fill a half. Measuring is real-world fraction work.

**Sharing snacks.** Ask your child to be the "fair-share chief". They cut, and others get to choose first, which encourages careful cutting.

**Paper folding.** Fold a strip of paper in halves, then quarters, then eighths, and colour one part each time. Line the strips up to compare: one half equals two quarters equals four eighths. This is the foundation of equivalent fractions, and the children discover it visually.

**Time.** Half past, quarter past and quarter to are fractions in everyday disguise. A clock face is a circle divided into fractions.

**Music.** Whole notes, half notes and quarter notes in music follow the same logic, if your child plays an instrument.

**Drawing games.** Draw a rectangle and ask your child to shade a third, or a quarter. Is it still a quarter if the parts are different shapes but the same area? (Yes, and this is a surprising idea that is worth exploring.)

## Be patient: fractions are a journey

Fractions are introduced over several years, and they are known to be one of the harder parts of the primary curriculum. That is not because children are weak, but because the ideas are rich: fractions are parts of wholes, parts of collections, points on a line, and results of division all at once. Children who are given time to explore the meaning with objects, pictures and conversation are much better placed to handle the procedures later.

So the next time you cut a sandwich in half, pause and ask your child, "How do we know they're equal?" It is a small question, but it opens the door to a lifetime of confident thinking about fractions.
